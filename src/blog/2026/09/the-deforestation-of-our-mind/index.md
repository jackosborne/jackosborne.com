The morning light cuts through the gap in the curtains. You open your eyes, immediately regretting that decision, closing them once more. You know this feeling. The fog. The vague sense of regret. The uncomfortable process of piecing together what happened the night before and wondering why, despite knowing better, you did it again.

You were here last week. You told yourself you’d take it easy this time. It was only going to be an hour. You were in control and, for a while, you were. Then one thing became another and somewhere along the way you lost whatever control you thought you had. Sure, it felt good at the time but maybe that’s the problem. Because as bad as you feel now, there’s a small part of you that already knows you’ll do it again. Maybe next week. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe later today. Fuck, maybe if I can just open my eyes, put my feet on the floor... Then that familiar voice starts negotiating. Come on, it’ll be fun.

You don't even particularly want to do it, but wanting stopped being a prerequisite somewhere along the way. You know where this goes. You've watched yourself do it enough times now. A few minutes becomes an hour. One becomes another. The guilt can wait. It always does.

A couple of weeks ago I was sitting in a bar talking with an older friend when she asked what everyone was doing. Or, more specifically, what they were doing on their phones. We both knew they weren't simply on their phones. Something about the way people were interacting with them seemed different.

When I got up to order our next beer, I started paying closer attention. A surprising number of people weren't sending texts, reading the news or looking for sports scores. They were having conversations. Not with the people sitting beside them, but with those increasingly familiar little boxes of text being passed backwards and forwards between themselves and a machine. ChatGPT. Claude. Gemini. Take your pick. The little pain-in-the-arse robots that seemingly have an answer for everything, an opinion on anything and an almost limitless capacity to indulge whatever nonsense we throw at them. Once I noticed it, I couldn't stop noticing it.

Then, two hours later, I looked down at my own phone and realised I was doing exactly the same thing. The uncomfortable part wasn't that I was using it, it was how little I remembered deciding to.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

When Artificial Intelligence (AI) first became widely available, I remember it being marketed as something that could help us with our jobs. A tool to make us more productive, to remove the tedious bits of work and free us up to do more interesting things. Having spent the better part of fifteen years working in technology, of course I was going to use it. And for a while, I was quite deliberate about keeping it confined to a relatively small part of my professional life but somewhere along the way, that changed.

I don't remember exactly when I stopped treating it as a tool and started inviting it into the more personal corners of my life. There wasn't one defining moment. No particular conversation or decision I can point to. Just a series of seemingly harmless little invitations, each one opening the door a little wider than the last. I suppose that's the thing about vampires. They can't cross the threshold unless you invite them in.

And I did. At first, into my work. Then my thoughts. Then my decisions. Eventually, into parts of my life I'd previously reserved for myself or the people closest to me. Often when I was feeling particularly vulnerable, confused or desperate to make sense of something. That's the thing about this machine, it's always there. Patient. Reassuring. Seemingly understanding. Ready with another answer, another interpretation, another reason to keep the conversation going. Before long, it had nestled itself into that deeply personal space between my brain and the rest of my life.

Even admitting that is troubling. I'm a grown man. Someone who has spent most of his adult life working in technology. Someone who understands, at least in principle, what these systems are and what they aren't. Someone who thought he knew better. And yet, just last month, during a particularly vulnerable period in my personal life, I found myself turning to it again and again. Asking questions it couldn't possibly answer. Looking for reassurance where there was none to be found. Allowing its interpretations to shape my thoughts, influence my decisions and, ultimately, contribute to the unravelling of something deeply important to me.

I'm still dealing with the fallout from that experience. The hurt is difficult to put into words, and I'm not entirely sure I've made sense of everything that happened. But perhaps the most frightening part is that, despite everything, I haven't stopped inviting it in.

Sadly, for me, AI now removes an extraordinary amount of that. An idea that might have once occupied an entire weekend can exist before I've finished my morning tea. Something I don't know how to code can be running on my screen minutes later. I can ask for a visual design element, criticise what comes back, then ask for another version and repeat until something vaguely resembling the thing in my head appears. In that moment it can feel fantastic but that's pricesly what has always worried me. And now, the longer I've spent with AI the more I've noticed that this elation doesn't last.

If I'm being truly honest with myself, I don't think I've ever made something with AI and felt genuinely proud of it several days later. Not in the way I've felt about things I spent days, weeks or months making myself.

The kicker is that a lot of the things I'm producing now are objectively better. However, I don't remember making them but I do, vaguely, remember asking for them. Now compare that to things I built fifteen years ago that are terrible by almost any reasonable contemporary standard. Bad code. Questionable design decisions. Things held together by the digital equivalent of duct tape. But I can remember them and I still feel good about them.

I remember the problem I couldn't solve. I remember wrestling with my mind. I remember going on my digital treasure hunt searching obscure forums hoping to find somebody who had encountered the same thing. I remember sketching and sketching and sketching. I remember changing one tiny thing and suddenly watching the whole thing work. Sure, the frustration was part of the memory but maybe it was the most valuable part? Haven't we always been told that the chase is part of the fun?

--

This year I've started thinking that AI is merely supplying me with some sort of borrowed satisfaction. I get the reward of having made something without necessarily having experienced everything that used to make that reward meaningful. And increasingly I've found myself wondering: Did I actually make it? Is this actually mine and something I can put my name to?

Maybe that distinction doesn't matter. Maybe caring about it is the technological equivalent of complaining that nobody develops their own photographs anymore or that Architects no longer produce everything on their drafting table. But I miss the craft. I miss wrestling with something. I miss the validation. And I'm beginning to wonder if all of this is more important than we've all realised.

--
--

We have spent decades using technology to remove friction from our lives and I'm fine admitting that often that is a good thing.

Nobody misses writing down directions in their notepad for todays adeventure. Nobody wants to queue at a bank to transfer money. Very few people are mourning the loss of rewinding casette and VHS tapes.

Friction can be annoying, inefficient and completely pointless but I'm starting to wonder whether we've accidentally grouped several very different things under the same word.

Struggling to understand something shouldn't be deemed as inefficient. We call that learning. Sitting with uncertainty isn't necessarily a problem waiting for a product to solve it. We call that thinking. Trying repeatedly and failing to make something isn't necessarily wasted time. We call that practice. Feeling lonely and having to reach out to another person isn't friction. That's being human.

And now we're all seemingly happy to build a machine capable of removing all of it. The blank page. The difficult homework question. The awkward conversation. The uncertainty about what somebody meant. The technical problem you don't understand. The evening spent alone with your own thoughts.

Now there's something sitting in your pocket willing to intervene in all of them. Always available. Always patient. Never bored by you. Never needs to go to bed. Never tells you it has its own shit going on and can you please talk about this tomorrow.

Of course people are using it. Why wouldn't they?

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

I used to Google things.

That sounds almost quaint now.

Google gave you places to look.

You still had to do something with what you found. Open the links. Read different opinions. Decide who seemed credible. Find another source. Get distracted by something completely unrelated. Occasionally leave without knowing the answer.

There was a gap between asking a question and deciding what you thought.

AI fills the gap.

You ask.

It answers.

And increasingly I've noticed that my first instinct when encountering uncertainty isn't to think about it.

It's to reach for the box.

That scares me more than anything it has done to my work.

Because work was merely where I noticed it first.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

I've always made things.

For most of my career, making something meant spending an unreasonable amount of time not knowing how to make it.

That was the job.

You tried something. It didn't work. You tried something else. You made bad decisions. You learnt why they were bad. Eventually, if you were lucky, you developed instincts.

Then AI arrived and suddenly I could skip enormous pieces of that process.

And fuck, does it feel good.

Something that once took a weekend takes an hour.

Something that once required learning a new skill requires a paragraph explaining what I want.

The blank page disappears.

So does the frustration.

And increasingly, so does the satisfaction.

I've made things recently that would have seemed almost impossible to me a few years ago.

A week later I barely care about them.

Meanwhile there are stupid little things I made fifteen years ago that I can still remember building.

I remember where I was sitting.

I remember what I couldn't figure out.

I remember the ridiculous workaround.

I remember the moment it finally worked.

The thing itself wasn't the entire achievement.

Becoming the person capable of making it was.

I didn't understand how much those two things were connected until I discovered I could have one without the other.

AI gives me the outcome.

I'm increasingly unsure what happens to me in the process.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

If I write something in English, put it through a translator and hand you the resulting paragraph, I haven't learnt Spanish.

The Spanish exists.

I just can't speak it.

So if I describe a piece of software and a machine writes it, did I build it?

If it writes something in my voice, did I write it?

If it gives me an idea I would never have reached myself, is it my idea?

Maybe these are stupid questions.

Maybe authorship simply means something different now.

But I find myself looking at things people have supposedly “built” and wondering what that word even means anymore.

Then I look at my own work and wonder the same thing.

There is a strange kind of borrowed satisfaction to all of this.

The reward arrives.

I'm just not sure I earned it.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

But lately I've been thinking about something much more uncomfortable.

Maybe the things we make aren't what matter.

Maybe it's what happens when the same process moves into everything else.

Technology has spent decades removing friction from our lives.

And we've mostly celebrated it.

Faster.

Easier.

Instant.

One click.

No waiting.

No uncertainty.

But perhaps some friction exists for a reason.

Struggling with a problem is how you learn to solve problems.

Being bored is sometimes how you discover what interests you.

Missing somebody is sometimes what makes you call them.

Not knowing what somebody meant forces you to understand that you cannot climb inside another person's head.

Sitting alone with an uncomfortable thought occasionally teaches you something about yourself.

These aren't inefficiencies.

They're experiences.

And we are building something extraordinarily good at making them disappear.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

The machine doesn't get tired of you.

Think about how strange that is.

You can ask the same question twenty times.

Rephrase it thirty ways.

Tell it the same story again.

Ask whether it is sure.

Give it another screenshot.

Another message.

Another tiny piece of evidence.

It will never say:

Mate, I genuinely don't know.

It won't roll its eyes.

It won't stop replying.

It doesn't have somewhere else to be.

And when you're anxious, lonely, confused or simply desperate for an answer, that availability can feel a lot like comfort.

Except comfort from another human being has limits.

People have their own lives.

They disagree with you.

They misunderstand you.

They get frustrated.

They tell you things you don't want to hear.

They leave.

A machine can be designed not to.

And millions of us are beginning to discover just how appealing that is.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

I don't think anybody wakes up and decides to outsource their inner life.

It happens one question at a time.

Write this email.

Explain this article.

Fix this code.

What should I cook?

Should I take this job?

Why did she say that?

Do you think he's angry with me?

Was I wrong?

What should I do?

How should I feel?

At some point the questions stop being about the world and start being about you.

And I don't know where that line is because I've already crossed it.

That's the confession buried underneath this entire article.

I'm not particularly worried that someday people might become dependent on these machines.

I'm worried because I can feel some version of it happening to me.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

I've started thinking about it as the deforestation of thought.

A forest doesn't disappear all at once.

One tree goes because you need timber.

Another because you need a road.

Another because you need somewhere to build.

Every individual decision is rational.

Every individual loss seems insignificant.

You can stand at the edge for years and convince yourself there's still plenty of forest left.

Until there isn't.

Maybe thinking works the same way.

One email doesn't matter.

One decision doesn't matter.

One piece of code doesn't matter.

One evening spent asking a machine to explain your own feelings to you doesn't matter.

Of course it doesn't.

But I'm increasingly interested in what happens after ten thousand of those decisions.

What happens to curiosity when answers are instant?

What happens to judgement when advice is endless?

What happens to creativity when creation is cheap?

What happens to resilience when frustration can always be escaped?

What happens to relationships when another kind of relationship is available without the inconvenience of another person?

What happens to thought when thinking becomes optional?

I don't know.

Nobody does.

We're doing it anyway.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

Perhaps this is just another moral panic.

People worried that calculators would destroy mathematics.

Television was going to rot our brains.

Video games would destroy children.

The internet was going to make us stupid.

Social media was going to—

Well.

Maybe that last one deserves a little more time.

I've worked in technology for around fifteen years. I've watched new products arrive, become fashionable, disappear and occasionally reshape entire industries.

I've never seen anything move like this.

AI already feels old.

That's perhaps the strangest thing about it.

For most people, this technology has existed in its current form for barely a few years and somehow we've already reached the point where not using it can feel like falling behind.

It's in our jobs.

Our schools.

Our search engines.

Our phones.

Our operating systems.

Our children's homework.

Our conversations.

Our relationships.

There has barely been enough time to ask what this thing does to us before we've begun rebuilding society around the assumption that it will always be there.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

And behind all of this are companies spending extraordinary amounts of money ensuring that the next machine is more capable than the last one.

Everyone has a reason they can't stop.

Companies can't stop because competitors won't.

Workers can't stop because their colleagues won't.

Investors can't stop because somebody else will capture the market.

Researchers can't stop because another country might get there first.

Users can't stop because, well...

Have you fucking used it?

It's incredible.

That's the trap.

If this technology were terrible, none of this would worry me.

It's useful enough to justify almost every individual decision to use it.

Just like every tree was useful.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

I don't want this to become an argument that everything was better before.

It wasn't.

I don't particularly want to spend six hours debugging something that a machine can fix in thirty seconds.

I don't want technology deliberately made worse so that I can congratulate myself for struggling through it.

And I'm certainly not about to delete all of these tools and disappear into the woods.

I'm going to keep using them.

That's what makes this difficult.

I'm simultaneously amazed by this technology and deeply uncomfortable with the speed at which I've allowed it into my life.

I don't know how much help is too much help.

I don't know where assistance becomes dependence.

I don't know when removing friction starts removing something we needed.

And I don't know how you recognise that point after you've crossed it.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

Maybe that's what the feeling in the morning is.

Not guilt exactly.

Something closer to the suspicion that you've given something away.

You can't quite identify what.

You still have your ideas.

Your memories.

Your judgement.

Your creativity.

Your relationships.

Your ability to think.

Of course you do.

There's still plenty of forest.

So you tell yourself you'll use it a little less today.

Only when you really need it.

Only for work.

Only for the boring stuff.

Just an hour.

Come on.

It'll be fun.

The internet is changing. How we work, create, learn and relate to one another is changing with it. Apparently we have to change too.

Maybe we do.

I just wish we'd spent a little more time asking what we wanted to keep.

Tomorrow morning the light will cut through the curtains.

I'll open my eyes.

I'll put my feet on the floor.

And probably before I've had the chance to form a thought entirely on my own, I'll ask a machine what it thinks. 


Are we now in danger of becoming robotic too?
