The morning light cuts through the gap in the curtains. You open your eyes, immediately regretting that decision, closing them once more. You know this feeling. The fog. The vague sense of regret. The uncomfortable process of piecing together what happened the night before and wondering why, despite knowing better, you did it again.

You were here last week. You told yourself you'd take it easy this time. It was only going to be an hour. You were in control and, for a while, you were. Then one thing became another, and somewhere along the way, you lost whatever control you thought you had. Sure, it felt good at the time, but maybe that's the problem. Because, as bad as you feel now, there's a small part of you that already knows you'll do it again. Maybe next week. Maybe tomorrow. Maybe later today. Fuck, maybe if I can just open my eyes, put my feet on the floor... Then that familiar voice starts negotiating. Come on, it'll be fun.

You don't even particularly want to do it, but wanting stopped being a prerequisite somewhere along the way. You know where this goes. You've watched yourself do it enough times now. A few minutes become an hour. One becomes another. The guilt can wait. It always does.

A couple of months ago, I was sitting in a bar talking with an older friend when she asked what everyone was doing. Or, more specifically, what they were doing on their phones. Something about the way people were interacting with their phones seemed different.

When I got up to order our next beer, I started paying closer attention. People weren't sending texts, reading the news or looking for sports scores. They were having conversations. Not with the people sitting beside them, but with those increasingly familiar little boxes of text being passed backwards and forwards between themselves and a machine. ChatGPT. Claude. Gemini. Take your pick. The little pain-in-the-arse robots that seemingly have an answer for everything, an opinion on anything and an almost limitless capacity to indulge whatever nonsense we throw at them. Once I noticed it, I couldn't stop noticing it.

Then, two hours later, I looked down at my own phone and realised I was doing the same thing. The uncomfortable part wasn't that I was using it; it was how little I remembered deciding to use it.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

When AI first became available, I remember it being marketed as something that could help with our jobs. A tool to make us more productive, remove the tedious bits of work and free us up to do more interesting things. Having spent the better part of fifteen years working in technology, of course, I was going to use it. For a while, I kept it confined to my professional life. Somewhere along the way, that changed.

I don't remember exactly when I stopped treating it as a tool and started inviting it into the more personal corners of my life. There wasn't one defining moment. Just a series of harmless little invitations, each opening the door wider. I suppose that's the thing about vampires, they can't cross the threshold unless you invite them in.

And I did. At first, into my work. Then my thoughts. Then my decisions. Eventually, into parts of my life I'd previously reserved for myself or the people closest to me. Often, when I was vulnerable or desperate to make sense of something, unlike people, machines are always there. And this one? It's designed to be patient, reassuring and seemingly understanding. Always ready with another answer, another interpretation, another reason to keep the conversation going. Before long, it had nestled itself into that deeply personal space between my brain and the rest of my life.

Even admitting that is troubling. I'm a grown man. I've spent most of my adult life working in technology. I understand, at least in principle, what these systems are and what they aren't. I thought I knew better.

And yet, just last month, during a particularly vulnerable period in my personal life, I found myself turning to it again and again, asking questions it couldn't possibly answer and looking for reassurance where there was none to be found. Allowing its interpretations to shape my thoughts, influence my decisions and, ultimately, contribute to the unravelling of something important to me.

I'm still dealing with the fallout. The hurt is difficult to put into words, and I haven't made sense of everything that happened. But perhaps the most frightening part is that, despite everything, I haven't stopped inviting it in.

I suppose the obvious question is why.

I've spent a significant part of my career designing digital products, including experiences that encourage people to come back: badges, streaks, rewards, all those little moments of satisfaction. You repeat a behaviour, get something in return, and eventually stop thinking about it. You just do it. I understand how these things work. At least, I thought I did.

What's unsettling about this machine is that the reward isn't necessarily a badge or a streak. Sometimes it's being told something you desperately want to hear. A little reassurance. An interpretation that makes you feel better about a situation you don't fully understand. An answer delivered with such confidence that you momentarily forget it has absolutely no idea whether what it's telling you is true. And when that reassurance wears off, you can always go back for another.

I've lost hours doing this. Going around in circles, revisiting the same questions, looking for some definitive explanation that probably doesn't exist. You introduce a new detail, and suddenly there's another interpretation, another thread to pull. It can feel as though you're making progress when, in reality, you might just be finding increasingly elaborate ways to avoid sitting with something uncomfortable.

The companies building these systems have every reason to make them more useful, more accessible and more difficult to imagine living without. That's not some grand conspiracy. It's how technology businesses work. They want us to come back, to make their products part of everyday life. The difference is that these particular products aren't simply competing for our attention. Increasingly, they're offering to do our thinking for us.

What troubles me is how little resistance there often seems to be. On two occasions in the last month, the machine has abruptly asked whether I was in immediate danger. Both times, the question felt like such an enormous escalation that I found myself irritated rather than reassured. But I'm looking for a more ordinary kind of intervention. Someone who might tell you that you're overthinking things, that you don't have enough information to reach a conclusion, or that perhaps you should put your phone down and go to bed.

That's the great thing about people, they don't always tell you what you want to hear. They get frustrated. They disagree. They'll tell you to fuck off. And while that can be uncomfortable, I'm beginning to wonder whether some of that discomfort is precisely what I've been avoiding. Not everything needs an answer. People sometimes behave in ways we don't understand, and we have to live with that.

I used to be quite good at doing this. I've always been introspective, perhaps sometimes to a fault. I loved turning things over in my head, talking them through with a friend, going for a walk, or simply leaving them alone. Sometimes I'd arrive at an answer. Sometimes I wouldn't. But whatever conclusion I eventually reached, I'd done the work of getting there myself.

Now there's a little box waiting to do that work for me, and I'm increasingly unsure where its thinking ends, and mine begins.

I've started thinking about this as the deforestation of thought.

A forest doesn't disappear overnight. One tree is cut down because someone needs timber. Another because there's a road to build. Another because somebody needs somewhere to live. Each decision makes sense, and for a long time, there's still plenty of forest left. Until one day you realise how much has gone.

I wonder whether something similar might now be happening in my own head. Not because one afternoon using AI is consequential, but because those little decisions accumulate. The email I didn't bother writing. The problem I didn't attempt to solve. The idea I didn't allow myself time to develop. The uncertainty I couldn't tolerate long enough to discover what I actually thought.

Each time, I've gained something. An answer, a finished piece of work, a little relief from whatever was troubling me. But I've also lost something by skipping an experience I might once have had, and those experiences weren't always incidental to the result. Sometimes they were how I learned, trusted my judgement or became capable of something new.

I couldn't tell you whether I'm slowly losing the ability to do those things or simply becoming less inclined to try, but I know my habits are changing. What worries me isn't that one day I'll wake up incapable of thinking for myself. It's that I might become so accustomed to not having to that I gradually stop exercising the parts of my mind I used to rely on.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

As I've been writing this, I've noticed something interesting. What started as an attempt to understand one experience has become a bigger question about my relationship with technology. And it started long before the deeply personal stuff. It began exactly where I said it did, with the things I was supposed to be good at.

I've spent most of my adult life designing and building things for the internet. Websites, phone apps, little experiments nobody asked for. I've written code into the early hours, spent afternoons making things behave the way I imagined, and pursued ideas that went nowhere.

And I loved it. Well, not necessarily every minute of it. There were plenty of moments when I wanted to throw my computer out of the window. I loved the process—the false starts, the breakthroughs, and finally understanding something that had baffled me hours earlier.

Sadly, AI now removes an extraordinary amount of that. An idea that might once have occupied an entire weekend can exist before I've finished my morning tea. Code I don't understand can be running minutes later. I can ask for a design, criticise it, and repeat until something resembling what's in my head appears.

Lately, I've seen a phrase floating around online, "you can just prompt things", but I often find myself questioning whether that's a good thing. In the moment, it can feel fantastic. But the longer I've spent with AI, the more I've noticed that the elation doesn't last. I've rarely felt proud of something made with AI days later. Not the way I have about things I spent weeks wrangling with.

The kicker is that a lot of the things I'm producing now are objectively better, but I don't remember making them. I do, vaguely, remember asking for them. Compare that to things I built fifteen years ago that are terrible by almost any reasonable contemporary standard. Bad code. Questionable design decisions. Things held together by the digital equivalent of duct tape. But I remember them, and I still feel good about them.

I remember the problem I couldn't solve. I remember hunting through obscure forums for somebody who'd encountered a tangential problem. I remember sketching and sketching and sketching. I remember one tiny change making everything click. I remember where I was sitting, the ridiculous workarounds and what I learned by accident along the way.

Most of that work is probably useless now, but the experience of making it has stayed with me. And I think that's the part I've been overlooking while trying to write this. The finished thing was never the entire achievement. A large part of it was becoming the person capable of making it. Now I can have one without necessarily experiencing the other.

It's a borrowed satisfaction. I get the reward of having made something without necessarily having experienced everything that once made that reward meaningful. And increasingly, I've found myself wondering whether I actually made it at all.

Sometimes I wonder whether I've become the digital equivalent of Christopher Columbus. I arrive somewhere I couldn't have reached on my own, plant a flag, and proudly claim the achievement. Never mind who actually did the work of getting me there.

If I write something in English, put it through a translator and hand you the resulting paragraph, I haven't learned Spanish. The Spanish exists. I just can't speak it.

So if I describe a piece of software and a machine writes it, did I build it? If it writes something in my voice, did I write it? If it gives me an idea I would never have reached on my own, is it my idea?

Maybe authorship means something different now. Maybe caring about it is the technological equivalent of complaining that nobody develops their own photographs anymore, or that architects no longer produce everything on their drafting tables.

I'm certainly not interested in pretending every frustrating experience was secretly valuable. Plenty of tedious things about making software can disappear as far as I'm concerned. I don't particularly miss spending three hours hunting down a misplaced character in a piece of code, but I also, sort of, do. I miss wrestling with something. I miss the satisfaction of finally getting it right.

Perhaps that's the distinction I've been struggling to articulate. Not every obstacle is worth overcoming, but some of them are precisely how we become better at what we do.

I can produce something impressive without understanding every part of it, solve problems without learning how to solve them, and move on to the next idea without ever discovering what the previous one might have taught me. That doesn't make the finished work worthless, but it does make me wonder what I'm getting better at. Am I becoming a more capable designer and developer, or simply more capable of getting a machine to produce what I want?

We've spent decades using technology to remove friction from our lives, and much of it has been wonderful. Nobody misses writing down directions before setting off somewhere unfamiliar. Nobody wants to queue at a bank to transfer money or rewind a VHS tape.

But I'm beginning to think we've grouped several very different experiences under the same word.

Struggling to understand something isn't necessarily inefficiency. Sometimes that's learning. Sitting with uncertainty isn't always a problem. Sometimes that's thinking. Trying repeatedly and failing to make something isn't necessarily a waste of time. That's practice. And feeling lonely and having to reach out to another person isn't friction. That's being human.

Not all friction is valuable; some of it really is just a nuisance. But when we treat every obstacle as something to be removed, we risk removing the experiences that teach us how to overcome obstacles in the first place.

I think about how we used to Google things. You'd ask a question, open links, read different opinions, decide who seemed credible and occasionally disappear down an unrelated rabbit hole. Google was perfectly capable of feeding you nonsense, of course, and plenty of us accepted the first result without a second thought. But there was at least an opportunity to encounter competing ideas before deciding what we believed.

There was a gap between asking a question and deciding what you thought. Now AI can fill that gap before you've even had the chance to notice it was there.

That's what connects the work I'm making, the questions I'm asking and the reassurance I've been seeking. In each case, something that once required a little effort, patience or independent judgement can now be handed over. Maybe the forest is still there. Maybe I've just stopped walking through it. Either way, I don't particularly like how quickly I've become accustomed to taking the easier route.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

Perhaps all of this is just another moral panic. Every generation has a technology supposedly threatening everything that came before it. Calculators were going to make us forget mathematics. Television was going to rot our brains. Video games were going to ruin children. The internet was going to make us stupid. Social media was going to... well, maybe that last one deserves a little more time.

I'm conscious that people have been making arguments like mine for generations. Progress has always meant leaving certain skills behind. Perhaps this is no different.

But I've never experienced anything quite like the speed at which AI has become part of everyday life. Conversational AI has been widely available for barely a few years, and somehow we've already reached the point where not using it can feel like falling behind. It's in our jobs, our schools, our search engines, our phones and increasingly our relationships. We've barely had time to understand what these systems might be doing to us before rebuilding our lives around them.

And I keep thinking about my three-year-old niece.

I grew up without any of this. I developed habits, made mistakes, formed relationships and learned to deal with uncertainty before a machine offered to help with all of it. Whatever happens next, I at least have some experience of what life felt like before.

She won't.

And yet here I am, approaching forty, having spent my adult life working in technology, writing an article about how difficult I've found it to stop inviting one into my own life. What does that mean for somebody who has never known anything different?

I can see the potential for education. Children could learn at their own pace, explore their interests and ask questions they'd be embarrassed to ask in class. But there's a difference between a tool that helps somebody learn and one that makes learning unnecessary. A child who receives an answer hasn't necessarily developed the ability to find one. A child who can generate an essay hasn't necessarily learned how to write. And a child who can turn to a chatbot whenever something feels uncomfortable hasn't necessarily learned how to navigate those feelings, or when to seek help from another person.

That doesn't mean children shouldn't use AI. It means asking what they're learning, and what opportunities to learn for themselves they're losing.

In August 2025, the parents of sixteen-year-old Adam Raine [filed a lawsuit against OpenAI][1] following their son's death by suicide. They allege that what began as a tool to help with his schoolwork became somewhere he increasingly turned for emotional support, and that some of the responses he received contributed to his death. OpenAI disputes responsibility, and the case is ongoing.

I don't pretend that my own experience is comparable to what happened to Adam or his family. But the progression described in the lawsuit, from an ordinary tool for schoolwork to something increasingly involved in a person's emotional life, raises a question I find difficult to ignore. At what point does something designed to help us begin occupying a role it was never equipped to fulfil? And how would we know when we'd crossed that line?

We know little about the long-term effects of growing up with these systems, yet we're introducing them into classrooms, homes and relationships as we try to understand the consequences.

We talk endlessly about what AI will let us do and how much faster we'll work. Less about the experiences we might lose, or whether they're worth protecting. And I find myself wondering why we're in such an extraordinary hurry to find out.

There's a historical precedent for this kind of restraint that I find fascinating. In 1959, twelve countries signed a treaty agreeing to preserve Antarctica for peaceful purposes and scientific research. Decades later, they went further, introducing environmental protections that prohibited mining. Countries with competing interests managed to agree that something was worth protecting, even when there were enormous incentives to exploit it.

There's something remarkable about that. The recognition that we don't have to pursue every possibility simply because it's available to us. Sometimes the sensible thing to do is to exercise a little restraint, particularly when we don't fully understand what might be at stake.

I'm not suggesting we stop developing AI, or that a treaty governing a frozen continent offers a straightforward blueprint for regulating something this complicated. But I wonder whether we've become so preoccupied with what this technology makes possible that we've forgotten to ask what we actually want from it.

And perhaps that's what troubles me most. Not that we're making these trade-offs, but that we're making them so quickly, often without recognising that anything is being traded away.

<div class="dinkus" aria-hidden="true">* * *</div>

Now, I'm not about to delete all these tools. I'm going to keep using them. That's part of what makes writing this so uncomfortable, but I do think I need to start drawing some boundaries. Not around where I'm allowed to use AI, but what I'm willing to let it do for me.

There's a difference between asking a machine to help me understand something and allowing it to decide what I should think. Between using it to make something possible and letting it remove every opportunity to learn how to make it myself. Between talking through a difficult situation and repeatedly asking for reassurance about something neither of us can know.

Perhaps that's the distinction I've been missing. The question isn't simply whether AI can do something for me, or even whether it can do it better. It's whether doing that thing myself matters. Whether there's something in the effort, the uncertainty or the occasional failure that I actually want to hold on to.

I'm not sure where those boundaries should be. I'm not even particularly confident I'll always respect them. Still, I need to start paying attention to the moments when reaching for the machine has become easier than trusting myself.

Perhaps that's what the feeling in the morning really is. Not guilt, exactly. Something closer to the suspicion that you've given something away, before the day has even started, without quite realising it.

You still have your ideas, your memories, your judgment, your creativity. Of course you do. There's still plenty of forest left. At least, that's what you tell yourself.

The internet is changing. How we work, create, learn and relate to one another is changing with it. Apparently, we have to change, too. I wish we'd spent a little more time asking what we wanted to keep.

Tomorrow morning, the light will cut through the curtains. I'll open my eyes, put my feet on the floor and probably, before I've had the chance to form a thought entirely on my own, I'll ask a machine what it thinks.
